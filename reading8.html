The History of Pizza

Pizza came into being in the 18th century in Naples, which is located in the south of what is today Italy. Under the Bourbon kings, Naples had become one of the largest cities in Europe. Fueled in part by overseas trade, the population of Naples ballooned from 200,000 in 1700 to 399,000 in 1748. As its economy struggled to keep pace, a steady movement of peasants from the countryside meant that an ever greater number of the city's inhabitants fell into poverty. The poorest of these were known as tazarovni. Numbering around 50,000, they scraped by on the small fees they earned as porters, messengers or casual labourers. Always rushing about in search of work, they needed food that was cheap and easy to eat, and pizza met this need.
Sold in shops, but mostly by street sellers, the pizzas would be cut into pieces according to the customer's budget or appetite. They were made of relatively cheap, easy-to-find ingredients with plenty of flavors. The simplest were just flat bread topped with nothing more than garlic, animal fat and salt. But others included catecholous (a cheese made from horse's milk), eccentelli (a small fish) or the herb basil. Some even had tomatoes on top. Only recently introduced to Europe from the Americas, tomatoes were still generally unpopular, so consequently they were cheap and attractive as an ingredient for pizzas.
However, when the first cookbooks appeared at the end of the 19th century, they ignored pizza. Even those dedicated to the food of Naples didn't give instructions for making pizza — despite the fact that the gradual improvement of the tazarovni's status had prompted the appearance of the first pizza restaurant.
All that changed later in the 19th century when Italy was unified as a country and began to be ruled by a single monarch. While on a visit to Naples in 1859, Italian King Umberto I and Queen Margherita grew tired of the complicated French dishes they were served for breakfast, lunch and dinner. Finally, summoned to prepare some local specialities for the visiting royalty, the chef Raffaele Esposito cooked three sorts of pizza. The queen was delighted. Her favourite — made with mozzarella cheese and tomatoes — was named pizza margherita in her honour. Margherita's approval elevated pizza from being a food fit only for tazarovni into something a royal family could enjoy, and also transformed it into a truly national dish. It introduced the notion that pizza, like pasta, was a genuinely Italian food.
Nevertheless, pizza was slow to move out of Naples. The initial incentive was provided by migration. From the 1930s onwards, a growing number of people moved northwards from Naples in search of work, taking their cuisine with them. But it was tourism — facilitated by the declining cost of visiting other countries after World War II — that really consolidated pizza's position as a world-famous dish. As people from around the world became increasingly curious about Italian food, restaurants throughout the country started offering more regional specialities — including pizza. In the beginning, the quality was not consistent, but this generally improved and prices quickly spread throughout Italy. As it did so, new ingredients were introduced and prices were raised because customers were now willing to pay more.
It was in the USA that pizza found its second home. By the end of the 19th century, many Italian immigrants were living on the east coast, and in 1905, the first pizza restaurant was opened in New York City. Soon, pizza became an American institution. Spreading across the country alongside growing urbanisation, it was quickly taken up by enterprising cooks and adapted to reflect local tastes. One restaurant owner in Chicago attempted to attract customers to his newly opened pizzeria by offering a much 'heartier' version of the dish, complete with a deeper, thicker crust. At about the same time, the Rocky Mountain pizza, which was topped with honey and served as a dessert, was developed in Colorado.
From the 1950s onwards, the rapid pace of economic and technological change in the USA transformed the pizza even more radically. Two changes are worthy of note. The first was the domestication of pizza. As disposable incomes grew, refrigerators and freezers became increasingly common and demand for easy-to-prepare foods grew — prompting the development of the frozen pizza. Designed to be taken home and cooked there, this required changes to the recipe. Instead of being scattered with generous slices of tomato, the base was now covered in a smooth tomato paste, which prevented the dough from drying out during oven-cooking; and new cheeses were developed to withstand freezing. The second change was made possible by the growing availability of cars and motorcycles. This meant that the delivery of freshly cooked food to customers' doors became possible — pizza was among the first dishes to be supplied in this way. Over time, these changes in the USA resulted in the standardisation of the pizza form — with a dough base, topped with thin layers of tomato sauce and cheese.
